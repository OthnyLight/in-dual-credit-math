## Summary
I analyzed public data on 359 Indiana public high schools and the 99,332 graduates from the 2021–2024 classes who enrolled at an Indiana public college. Three findings bear on the Commission's review.

1. **Dual credit's readiness return at the school level is modest at most.** Schools where more graduates earn dual credit send students with somewhat higher first-year GPAs and more credits, even after accounting for school poverty, AP success, Algebra I timing, and math offerings. Moving from a low-uptake school to a high-uptake school (46 percentage points) corresponds to at most about 0.06 GPA points and 0.8 credits. Roughly 80% of the raw advantage reflects differences between the schools that offer dual credit and those that do not. Part of the rest is mechanical: the first-year GPA and credits the state reports include dual-credit grades and hours earned in high school. Remediation, which is not affected by that overlap, shows no clear relationship. The Commission could separate these by reporting first-year outcomes that exclude credit earned before enrollment.

2. **The gap between dual-credit earners and their classmates is widest where programs are largest.** Within a school, dual-credit earners average 0.56 GPA points higher in their first college year than classmates who did not earn dual credit (partly because their dual-credit grades count), and that gap is wider in schools where dual credit is more common. As programs grow, the remaining students are the least prepared, and they are the ones whose outcomes have the most room to improve.

3. **The mathematics pathway into college credit is missing where poverty is highest.** 77 of 381 traditional public high schools (20%) offered no calculus in either of the last two federal civil-rights collections (2021-22, 2023-24). That rate is 5% in the lowest-poverty quarter of schools and 41% in the highest. 40% of city high schools offered no calculus, and in 3 counties no public high school did. 19 schools reported no dual enrollment at all, and 20 more had neither calculus nor substantial dual-credit uptake.

## Recommendations
1. **Report dual credit by subject.** State data identify whether a graduate earned dual credit, not in what subject. Adding subject (at minimum, mathematics and English) to CHE's high school reports would let the state see where college-level math is reached and where it is not.
2. **Target capacity grants at the schools without a math pathway.** The schools identified here lack the sequence that feeds college-level mathematics. Support for credentialing a mathematics teacher, sharing a section across schools, or a college-taught online section would reach students the current expansion misses. In my own district, when Lawrence Central briefly had no approved finite-mathematics teacher, its students could take the course only because a sister school nearby had one; most of the schools on this list have no such fallback.
3. **Measure success by who is reached, not only by how many credits are earned.** Because outcomes for non-participants are weakest where programs are largest, state goals should track the share of graduates, by school poverty level, who complete a college-level mathematics course before graduation.

## Method and materials
The analysis uses only public data: CHE College-Going datasets (2019 and 2021–2024 cohorts), the U.S. Department of Education Civil Rights Data Collection (2021-22 and 2023-24), and NCES Common Core of Data. Associations are school-level and observational; they are not estimates of the effect of dual credit on individual students. Code, data, an interactive map of every school, and a full manuscript are available at https://github.com/OthnyLight/in-dual-credit-math.

I would welcome the opportunity to share the school-level map with Commission staff.
